Research

My research investigates how digital technologies shape identity, culture, and psychological well-being, with a specific focus on Black communities. I combine quantitative and qualitative methods to study both the affordances and the limitations of digital systems for meeting core human needs for belonging, affirmation, and care.

Dissertation

Ph.D. in Psychology, Certificate in Digital Studies · Expected Spring 2027 · University of Michigan, Ann Arbor

Black Digital Lifeworlds: A Mixed Methods Approach to Exploring the Psychological Impacts of Identity, Culture, Technology, and Well-being

Advisors: Isis Settles, L. Monique Ward

My dissertation develops Black Digital Lifeworlds as both a theoretical framework and an analytic framework for understanding how Black people make meaning, build relationships, and sustain life under technological conditions. This work integrates phenomenology with Social Identity Theory to examine Black Digital Culture as a lived cultural practice. Study 1 employed interpretive phenomenological analysis of focus groups with 19 Black emerging adults (ages 18–30) to map the dimensions of everyday BDC practice. Participants described how humor, coded language, and everyday camaraderie function as strategies for cultivating joy and connection in the face of racialized trauma and algorithmic marginalization. Study 2 utilized a cross-sectional survey of 1,074 Black adults (ages 18–74), utilizing novel measures: the Black Digital Culture Engagement Scale (BDCES), which captures the frequency and diversity of BDC participation, and the BDC Literacy Scale, which assesses interpretive competence in understanding culturally-specific digital content. In Study 3, I employed an experimental design using memes as cultural stimuli to establish causal evidence. Black participants (N = 534) exposed to BDC memes (vs. racially neutral control memes) reported significantly higher state well-being and a stronger sense of belonging, demonstrating that even brief encounters with culturally resonant digital content produce immediate affective benefits. This work addresses a critical methodological gap in existing social media research that conflates access (time on platforms) with meaning (cultural engagement), often rendering Black digital practices invisible or pathological.
