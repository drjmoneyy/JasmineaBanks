Research

My research investigates how digital technologies shape identity, culture, and psychological well-being, with a specific focus on Black communities. I combine quantitative and qualitative methods to study both the affordances and the limitations of digital systems for meeting core human needs for belonging, affirmation, and care.

Dissertation

Ph.D. in Psychology, Certificate in Digital Studies · Expected Spring 2027 · University of Michigan, Ann Arbor

Black Digital Lifeworlds: A Mixed Methods Approach to Exploring the Psychological Impacts of Identity, Culture, Technology, and Well-being

Advisors: Isis Settles, L. Monique Ward

This project uses mixed methods to examine how Black people experience digital environments as sites of identity construction, cultural meaning, and psychological consequence. The dissertation treats participant accounts as evidence in their own right, situating quantitative measurement of well-being alongside qualitative description of lived digital experience.

Research Interests

  • Black digital culture. How Black users create, circulate, and defend cultural meaning within digital spaces not designed with their experiences in mind.
  • Meaning-making and well-being. How people construct significance and psychological stability through digital practice, and what digital systems do to support or undermine that construction.
  • AI ethics and sociotechnical governance. The ethical and structural questions raised by automated and algorithmic systems, particularly where those systems affect marginalized users.
  • Online dating. The psychological and relational dynamics of digital intimacy, including how racialized and gendered experience shapes those dynamics.
  • Human-technology relations. The broader question of how humans and digital systems shape one another over time, addressed through a phenomenological lens.

Method

My approach draws on mixed-methods design in psychology, pairing quantitative analysis with qualitative and phenomenological inquiry. I treat first-person accounts of digital experience as primary evidence, not as illustration for quantitative findings. This means research questions are built from what people report about their own lives, and measurement tools are evaluated against the accuracy with which they capture that reported experience.
